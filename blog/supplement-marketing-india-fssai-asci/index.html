Marketing a Supplement Brand in India Without Breaking FSSAI and ASCI Rules

By Digital Dobby · 17 November 2026 · 7 min read

Supplement and wellness brands are among the fastest-growing D2C categories in India, and among the most likely to have ads rejected, influencer posts flagged or claims challenged. The rules aren’t there to stop you marketing. They stop you promising what the product can’t prove.

This guide covers the main rules in plain language and how to market well within them. It isn’t legal advice: for specific claims, check with a food regulatory consultant or lawyer.

1. FSSAI: what you can claim

Health supplements and nutraceuticals in India fall under FSSAI. Their advertising follows the Food Safety and Standards (Advertising and Claims) Regulations, 2018, and the 2022 health supplement regulations work with them for all claims. In practice:

  • No disease claims. A supplement can’t claim to prevent, treat or cure a disease. “Supports healthy digestion” is a different kind of statement from “cures acidity”.
  • Claims must be backed. Nutrition and health claims need scientific support. Claims outside the permitted lists need prior approval from FSSAI.
  • No misleading words. Words like “natural”, “pure” or “fresh” in a brand or product name come with labelling rules, including a disclaimer.

FSSAI actively monitors ads: its Advertising Monitoring Committee has reported many cases of misleading claims. Read the regulations on fssai.gov.in.

Separately, the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act prohibits ads that claim to treat certain listed diseases and conditions. Another reason to keep disease language out of your marketing.

2. ASCI: honest ads and influencers

The Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) code applies to all ads, including social media. Two parts matter most for supplement brands:

  • Disclosure. Paid influencer posts must carry a clear label such as “#ad” or “Collaboration” that people notice, not buried among hashtags.
  • Qualified voices for technical health claims. Since ASCI’s April 2025 update, influencers only need to be qualified (doctor, nutritionist, dietitian and similar) and state that qualification when they give technical health or nutrition advice, such as prevention, treatment or cure claims. General content about healthy habits doesn’t need it.

Brands are responsible too: ASCI expects you to make sure the influencers you hire follow the guidelines. See the ASCI website for the current code.

3. Meta and Google ad policies

Ad platforms add their own rules on top of the law. Expect closer review for supplements, especially weight loss and “hormonal” products:

  • Avoid before-and-after images and anything that makes people feel bad about their bodies.
  • Don’t imply you know someone’s condition (“Struggling with PCOS?” addressed to the viewer).
  • Keep landing page claims consistent with the ad, since platforms review both.

How to market well within the rules

Compliant marketing doesn’t have to be weak marketing. What works:

  1. Sell the routine, not the cure. Talk about the habit and the experience: taste, convenience, ingredients, how it fits a morning.
  2. Lead with transparency. Ingredients, sourcing, lab testing and certifications build trust with today’s careful buyers.
  3. Use real customer stories carefully. Reviews about taste, routine and service are safe. Remove reviews that make medical claims.
  4. Win on SEO and content. Helpful guides (“how to read a supplement label”, “protein needs for vegetarians”) bring in shoppers searching for answers, without paid-ad review issues.
  5. Build retention. Subscriptions, reminder emails and bundles grow revenue from customers you already have.

That’s the approach we took with Jollywell, a plant-based supplement brand: SEO and content, continuously A/B-tested ads and re-engagement emails, for 45% more organic traffic and 25% better ad conversion rates.

A quick pre-publish checklist

  • Does any line suggest the product prevents, treats or cures a disease?
  • Can you back every health or nutrition claim?
  • Is every paid influencer post clearly labelled?
  • Does any influencer give technical health advice without stating a relevant qualification?
  • Do the ad and the landing page say the same thing?
